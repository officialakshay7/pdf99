The Best Free PDF Tools for Students in 2026

July 28, 2026 6 min read By PDF99 Team

Students deal with PDFs constantly — lecture slides, academic papers, coursework submissions, university forms, reading lists, revision materials. The problem is that most PDF tool recommendations online are aimed at business users who don't mind paying monthly subscriptions. This guide focuses on what students actually need and how to get it for free.

The five PDF tasks students need most

Before getting into specific tools, here's what students actually need from PDF software, in order of how often they come up:

  1. Compressing PDF files — lecture slides from Moodle or Canvas are often huge; assignment portals cap uploads at 5MB or less
  2. Merging PDFs — combining multiple assignment files into one submission, or assembling research materials
  3. Splitting/extracting pages — getting specific chapters from a large PDF, or extracting the pages that matter from a 200-page textbook
  4. Converting to PDF — turning scanned notes, photos of whiteboards, or image-based figures into a submittable PDF
  5. Annotating and signing — adding comments, signing consent forms, completing digital forms

Compressing lecture slides and notes

University lecture slides created in PowerPoint or Keynote can be enormous — 50MB for a 50-slide lecture deck isn't unusual when high-resolution images are embedded. Reading week is painful if you're downloading 20 of these on a slow connection.

The PDF99 Compress PDF tool handles this well. For lecture slides, a quality setting of 70-80% produces compressed files that are 30-60% smaller with no visible degradation when reading on a laptop screen. The DPI control lets you target 96-120 DPI for screen reading rather than the default 150 DPI used for print, which reduces file sizes further.

If you need to upload a file to a Turnitin, Canvas, or Moodle submission portal with a specific size cap, the targeted compression tools — Compress to 200KB, Compress to 500KB — automatically find the right quality setting to hit the limit.

Merging assignment components

Many assignments require submitting multiple components as a single PDF: a cover sheet, the main body, appendices, and reference list. Some students write each section in a separate document and need to combine them before submission.

The PDF99 Merge PDF tool combines multiple PDFs in seconds. Add them in order, check the thumbnail sequence, click merge. There's no limit on the number of files or total page count, which matters when combining a dissertation with multiple appendices.

One useful workflow: write each major section in Google Docs, export each to PDF, then merge the section PDFs into the final submission document. This makes collaborative writing straightforward (different authors can work on different sections) and produces a cleanly structured final submission.

Extracting chapters and specific pages

Journal articles are often distributed as part of larger compiled PDFs — conference proceedings, journal issues, edited volumes. You want the 30-page article, not the 400-page surrounding document.

The PDF99 Split PDF tool is ideal here. Upload the large PDF, see thumbnails of every page, select the pages you want (click thumbnails or type a range like 45-74), and extract them as a single PDF or individual pages. This is also useful for revision — extracting just the chapters relevant to an upcoming exam rather than carrying around the full textbook.

Converting photos of notes and whiteboards

After a lecture, photographing the whiteboard or a page of your handwritten notes with your phone gives you an image, not a PDF. Converting that image to a PDF before importing it into Notability, GoodNotes, or your revision folder keeps everything consistent.

The PDF99 JPG to PDF tool takes multiple images and combines them into a PDF. If you've photographed 10 whiteboard panels from a maths lecture, you can combine all 10 into one numbered PDF in under a minute. The tool lets you rotate images (for photos that ended up sideways), set the page size, and choose whether to put each image on its own page or scale them to fit.

Annotating and signing forms

Universities generate a lot of forms that need signatures — learning support agreements, extenuating circumstances forms, ethics approval forms, dissertation supervision records. Printing, signing, and scanning is annoying. An electronic signature achieves the same result faster.

On a Mac, Preview handles this natively and well (see the guide on signing PDFs online for detailed steps). On Windows, the free tier of Adobe Acrobat online handles a limited number of signings per month, which is usually enough for student use.

Managing your PDF library

One overlooked aspect of being a student is keeping your PDF collection organised. By the end of a semester you can have hundreds of PDFs across multiple modules. A few practices that help:

Name files consistently from the start: Module-Topic-Week or Author-Title-Year makes searching much faster. Use subfolders per module and per assessment. Keep a "to-read" folder separate from "read" so you can see what you've actually covered. Merge related readings into one file where they form a coherent unit — a week's readings on the same topic merge into one browsable document more usefully than five separate files.
