How to Scan Documents to PDF on Android

July 21, 2026 5 min read By PDF99 Team

Turning a physical document into a PDF using only your Android phone has become genuinely good — modern phone cameras combined with built-in scanning software produce results that are clear, properly proportioned, and appropriately compressed. You don't need a flatbed scanner for most document scanning tasks anymore.

Method 1: Google Drive's built-in scanner (fastest)

Every Android phone with Google Drive installed — which is essentially every Android phone outside of China — has a document scanner built in. It's one of the best available because Google's processing automatically corrects perspective, enhances contrast, and outputs a properly sized PDF.

  1. Open Google Drive on your Android phone
  2. Tap the blue + button in the bottom right corner
  3. Tap "Scan" — a camera view opens with document detection enabled
  4. Position the document in the frame. Drive automatically detects document edges and waits for the image to be stable before capturing
  5. Review the scan — you can retake it or crop/adjust the detected edges
  6. Add more pages if needed (for multi-page documents)
  7. Tap Save — the document is saved as a PDF directly to your Google Drive

The output is a compressed PDF with the text layer preserved — meaning the PDF is searchable (you can Ctrl+F for text in it) rather than just an image.

Method 2: Samsung Notes or Samsung's built-in scan

Samsung phones have scan capabilities built into multiple places. The most accessible is through Samsung Notes:

  1. Open Samsung Notes (pre-installed on all Samsung phones)
  2. Create a new note and tap the attachment icon
  3. Choose "Add attachments" then "Scan"
  4. Scan your pages and the result is embedded in the note
  5. Export the note as a PDF

Alternatively, Samsung phones from the Galaxy S series onwards have a "Scan documents" shortcut in the camera app — accessible by swiping left in the camera viewfinder to reach the "More" modes section.

Method 3: Microsoft Lens (best for paper documents)

Microsoft Lens is a free app available on Android that's specifically designed for document scanning. It's particularly good at handling white paper in variable lighting conditions:

  1. Install Microsoft Lens from the Play Store (free)
  2. Select "Document" mode
  3. Hold your phone over the document — Lens automatically detects and captures when the document is in frame
  4. Adjust edges if needed
  5. Export directly to PDF, OneDrive, Word, or your phone's gallery

Microsoft Lens also has a "Whiteboard" mode that's excellent for photographing handwritten notes, meeting notes on whiteboards, and hand-drawn diagrams.

Getting the file size right after scanning

A common problem with phone-scanned documents is file size. Google Drive typically produces well-compressed output, but other scanning methods can produce large files — a 10-page document might scan to 8-15MB. If you need to send it by email or upload it to a portal with a size limit, compression is necessary.

After saving your scanned PDF to your phone, open Chrome on Android and go to pdf99.org/compress-pdf. Upload the scanned PDF and use the quality slider to compress it. A quality setting of 60-75% typically reduces scanned documents by 60-80% while keeping text completely legible. The compressed file saves back to your phone and is ready to share.

Tips for getting clear scans

Lighting is the most important factor. Natural light from a window — positioned so it illuminates the document without creating glare — produces the best scans. Avoid scanning in dim artificial light, which forces the camera to increase ISO and introduces noise that makes text look grainy in the output.

Flatten the document before scanning. A slightly curled page creates curved text that looks poor in the output even after perspective correction. For important documents, place them on a flat surface and use something light to hold down the edges if they're curling.

Hold the phone parallel to the document surface — directly above it, not at an angle. Automatic perspective correction handles modest angles well, but extreme angles produce distorted output that no amount of correction can fully fix.
