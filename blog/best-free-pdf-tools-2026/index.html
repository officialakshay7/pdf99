The Best Free PDF Tools in 2026

May 1, 20266 min readBy PDF99 Team

The "free" PDF tool space is full of traps. Tools that watermark your output. Tools that are free for two conversions per day. Tools that demand your email address just to download your file. We spent time testing what's actually available and separated the genuinely useful from the gotcha-laden.

What to look for in a free PDF tool

Beyond the tools themselves, it's worth having a checklist when evaluating any free PDF tool you encounter. First: does it add a watermark? Test this immediately — download any output file and open it. A watermark in the footer or corner is a deal-breaker for professional use. Second: does it require an account before downloading? Some tools let you process the file but gate the download behind a registration wall. That's not free, that's a lead generation funnel. Third: are there hidden conversion limits? "Free" tools that cap usage at 2-3 conversions per day aren't meaningfully free for anyone with regular needs.

Fourth: where does your file go? Any tool that processes files server-side sends your document to a remote server. For sensitive documents — contracts, financial records, personal information — this matters. Browser-based tools that process locally (like PDF99) remove this concern entirely. Fifth: does the tool actually do what it says? Test with a realistic file, not a simple 1-page test PDF. A tool that claims to compress PDFs but only strips metadata without touching images is much less useful than one that genuinely re-encodes embedded images.

Free tools that cover edge cases

Beyond the mainstream tools, a few free options deserve mention for specific scenarios. Sejda offers a generous free tier (3 tasks per hour, files up to 50MB) with a clean interface and server-side processing. Good for tasks that browser-based tools struggle with. PDF24 is genuinely free with no watermarks and offers a wider range of tools than most competitors, though it requires upload to their servers. Preview on Mac remains one of the most powerful free PDF tools available — it handles merging, splitting, annotation, signing, and even basic compression through its Quartz filter system, all without any third-party tool. If you're on a Mac, explore Preview's capabilities before reaching for an online tool.

For OCR specifically — making scanned PDFs searchable — Google Drive provides excellent free OCR simply by opening any scanned PDF in Google Docs. The recognised text is embedded back into the PDF when you export. The quality is good enough for most purposes at no cost and without installing anything.

The case for using multiple tools

No single tool does everything best. The practical approach for most people is a small toolkit of 2-3 free tools used for specific purposes. PDF99 for browser-based conversion, compression, merging, and splitting where privacy matters. Preview (Mac) or a desktop tool for tasks requiring more control. Google Drive for OCR. This multi-tool approach gives you the best of each without any cost and without committing to one service that might change its pricing or feature set.

The goal is to have a reliable answer to every common PDF task without reaching for Adobe Acrobat's paid tier. That's entirely achievable in 2026 with the tools described in this guide.

What makes a PDF tool genuinely free?

The word 'free' gets misused constantly in the software world. Before trusting any tool with your documents, it's worth checking a few things. First, does it add a watermark to your output? Many tools that advertise themselves as free add a small but permanent watermark to every page — often in the footer or corner. Your client or employer sees that watermark. Second, does it cap the number of conversions? A tool that gives you three free uses per day before demanding payment isn't free — it's a trial. Third, does it require an account or email address just to download your file? That's a data collection strategy disguised as a free product. A genuinely free tool gives you unlimited output with no watermarks and no registration. That standard is harder to find than it should be, but it's what you should expect.

Converting images to PDF is one of the most common tasks, especially for people who photograph documents or need to submit image files through portals that only accept PDFs. The best free option is a browser-based converter that accepts multiple image formats at once — JPG, PNG, WebP, HEIC — and lets you arrange them before combining. PDF99's JPG to PDF tool does this entirely in your browser using jsPDF, meaning your photos never leave your device. You can drag thumbnails to reorder pages, rotate individual images, and choose the output page size. Single image or 50 images — it handles both.

Best free tool for PDF compression

Compression is where most free tools either don't go far enough or go too far and produce unusable output. The best approach is one that gives you control over the quality-to-size trade-off rather than applying a single fixed compression level. A quality slider lets you choose how aggressively to compress — 85% for near-invisible changes, 60% for significant size reduction. PDF99's compression tool also lets you set DPI separately, which is particularly useful for scanned documents where reducing the image resolution from 300 to 150 DPI halves the file size with minimal visible difference on screen.

Best free tool for merging PDFs

The key feature for a merge tool isn't just combining files — it's the ability to reorder them. Drag-and-drop thumbnail reordering before merging means you can add files in any order and arrange them correctly before committing. PDF99's merge tool uses PDF-lib to copy pages directly without re-rendering, which means no quality loss regardless of how many files you combine. There's no limit on the number of documents or total page count. The output is a clean single PDF that behaves like any other — searchable, printable, and compatible with every PDF viewer.

Best free tool for splitting and extracting pages

Page extraction is something most people don't need often, but when they need it they really need it. A good split tool shows you visual thumbnails of every page so you can see what you're extracting without opening a separate viewer. Range input — typing something like 5-12 or 1,3,7 — is faster than clicking individual pages when you know exactly which pages you want. PDF99's split tool offers both thumbnail clicking and range input, and lets you choose between downloading selected pages as individual PDFs in a ZIP or as a single merged document.

Tools worth knowing for specific tasks

Beyond the core four, there are tasks that come up less often but need handling. Rotating PDFs — when scanned documents come out sideways — is quick and lossless with PDF99's rotate tool. Watermarking is useful for anyone sharing draft documents or protecting previews; the watermark tool places diagonal text at adjustable opacity on every page. For anyone who needs to share specific pages of a document without the whole thing, the split tool makes it trivial to extract exactly the pages you want.

The honest limitation of browser-based PDF tools

It's worth being clear about where free browser-based tools reach their limits. Complex PDF editing — changing existing text, moving images, modifying form fields in a sophisticated way — is genuinely difficult in a browser environment and the results are often imperfect. If you routinely need to make substantial edits to existing PDFs, a desktop application like LibreOffice or a professional tool like Adobe Acrobat is more appropriate. Browser tools are best for conversion, compression, combining, splitting, and annotation — not for heavy editing. Knowing that boundary saves a lot of frustration.
